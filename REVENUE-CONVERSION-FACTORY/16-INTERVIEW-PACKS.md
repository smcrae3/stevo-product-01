# 16 — INTERVIEW PREP FACTORY

Five packs, ten questions each, with truthful short answers.

**The through-line in every answer:** Steven is not a technical person
pretending to be more technical. He is an **operations and communication
person** who has learned to use AI as a production tool and who verifies
everything. That story is true, it is coherent, and it is more valuable in
2026 than a thin technical claim.

**`[BRACKETS]` = Steven inserts his own specifics.** Answers are frameworks,
not scripts to memorize — memorized answers sound memorized.

# PACK 1 — PROCESS DOCUMENTATION / SOP WRITER

**1. "Walk me through how you'd document a process you know nothing about."**
> Watch it done first — screen recording, narrated. Then interview the person
> who actually does it, not their manager, because the manager describes it as
> designed and the doer knows the exceptions. I ask two questions that produce
> most of the value: *what goes wrong most often*, and *what would you tell a
> new person on day one that isn't written anywhere*. Then I draft, walk every
> step myself, and test it on someone who doesn't know the process. Wherever
> they get stuck, I rewrite — the document is wrong, not the reader.

**2. "What makes a bad SOP?"**
> It documents the happy path only. Every real process has exceptions, and
> the exceptions are exactly when someone reaches for the SOP. Second most
> common: it's written by someone who never did the work, so it skips the
> step that only makes sense if you already know the workaround.

**3. "How do you handle a subject-matter expert who won't give you time?"**
> Make it cheap for them. Ask for a 20-minute recording instead of a meeting —
> they narrate while they work, no prep. Then I come back with a draft and
> specific questions rather than open ones. Reviewing a draft takes ten
> minutes; explaining from scratch takes an hour. I do the work; they check it.

**4. "How do you know your documentation is good?"**
> Someone who doesn't know the process follows it without asking me anything.
> That's the only test that matters. Everything else is opinion.

**5. "What tools do you use?"**
> Google Docs and Sheets, screen recording, standard annotation tools. I use
> AI for transcription and a first structural draft, then verify every step
> myself in the actual system. I haven't used Confluence or MadCap in a
> professional setting — I'd expect to pick either up quickly, but I'd rather
> tell you that than imply otherwise.

**6. "Tell me about a time you improved a process."** *(Have two written out.)*
> Structure: what was broken → what I noticed → what I did → what it saved.
> Use a real T-Mobile example. Concrete numbers if he can defend them,
> honest estimates if not.

**7. "You don't have technical writing experience."**
> Not by that title. I have [X] years of explaining complicated things to
> people who were already frustrated, which is the harder version of the same
> skill. I brought samples so you can judge the output rather than the job
> title — they're demonstrations, not client work, and I'll tell you exactly
> what I did on each.

**8. "How do you handle documenting a process that's actively changing?"**
> Version it and date it clearly, document the current state, and flag which
> sections are volatile so readers know what to double-check. Documenting a
> moving process is still better than nothing — but I'd want to agree upfront
> who tells me when it changes, or it goes stale silently, which is worse
> than no documentation because people trust it.

**9. "How long does an SOP take you?"**
> A single process from a recorded walkthrough: two to four hours including
> verification and reader-testing. Longer if I have to chase the SME or if
> the process branches a lot. I'd rather quote honestly and deliver early.

**10. "Why are you moving from customer service into this?"**
> I spent years fixing the same problems over and over. At some point you
> realize you're treating symptoms — the actual problem is upstream, in how
> the process is defined and whether anyone wrote it down. I'd rather work
> on that than keep apologizing for it.

# PACK 2 — OPERATIONS ASSISTANT / COORDINATOR

**1. "How do you prioritize competing urgent tasks?"**
> Ask what breaks if each one waits. Most "urgent" things have different real
> deadlines than stated ones. I sort by consequence and dependency, then tell
> whoever's waiting when they'll get their thing — people accept a later
> answer far better than silence.

**2. "Describe a time you organized something chaotic."**
> [Real T-Mobile example.] Structure: what the mess was → the structure I
> imposed → what it enabled.

**3. "What's your Excel/Sheets level?"**
> Comfortable and practical rather than advanced. Lookups, QUERY,
> ARRAYFORMULA, pivots, validation, cleanup and dedupe. I'm not writing
> complex macros unaided, and I use AI assistance for Apps Script and then
> test it thoroughly. I'd rather be precise about that than have you find
> out in week two.

**4. "How do you handle a manager who gives unclear instructions?"**
> Play it back before starting: *"So I'm doing X, by Y, and success looks
> like Z — is that right?"* Thirty seconds up front saves a day of wrong
> work. That's a habit from customer service, where assuming what someone
> meant is how you get an escalation.

**5. "Tell me about a mistake you made."**
> Real one, honestly told: what happened → immediate impact → what I did →
> what I changed so it couldn't recur. **Never "I work too hard."** The
> question tests self-awareness, not perfection.

**6. "How do you handle repetitive work?"**
> Do it, then look at whether it should exist. Repetitive work is usually a
> signal about the process. I've built Forms-to-Sheets workflows with
> automated confirmations specifically to remove manual re-entry — but I
> finish the task first and propose the change second.

**7. "What does a good week look like to you?"**
> Nothing dropped, no one waiting on me for something I forgot, and at least
> one thing that used to be manual now isn't.

**8. "How do you handle confidential information?"**
> Handled customer account and billing data daily under company policy.
> Practically: least access necessary, don't move data out of approved
> systems, redact before sharing, and ask when unsure rather than assume.

**9. "Why part-time/contract?"**
> Straight answer: [Steven's real reason — currently employed, building
> toward operations work, wants to contribute in the area he's strongest in].
> Honesty here reads better than a manufactured career narrative.

**10. "Where do you want to be in two years?"**
> Doing business analysis or implementation work — the requirements and
> process side. This role is directly on that path, which is why I applied
> to it specifically rather than broadly.

# PACK 3 — IMPLEMENTATION COORDINATOR / ONBOARDING

**1. "Walk me through how you'd onboard a new client."**
> Kickoff to confirm what they actually bought and what success looks like.
> Gather access and requirements early, because that's what stalls
> implementations. Set milestones with named owners on both sides. Communicate
> proactively — most implementation frustration is uncertainty, not delay.
> Document as I go so handover isn't a scramble. Close with a check that
> they can actually use the thing.

**2. "How do you handle a client who wants something out of scope?"**
> Say yes to the person, not necessarily to the request. *"That's a good idea
> and it's outside what we scoped — let me find out what it'd take."* Never a
> flat no in the moment and never a silent yes. Silent yeses are how projects
> die.

**3. "Have you implemented [platform]?"**
> Not that one. I've built and tested workflows in Google Workspace,
> Apps Script with AI assistance, and Make.com. What I'm actually good at is
> the part before configuration — sitting with a customer and working out what
> they need, then explaining what we built in language they can use. Platform
> specifics I can learn; that conversation is the harder half.

**4. "How do you gather requirements from someone who doesn't know what they want?"**
> Ask about the problem, not the solution. *"Walk me through how you do this
> today. What's the worst part?"* People describe their pain accurately even
> when they describe their desired solution badly. Then I play it back as
> must-have, nice-to-have, and out of scope, and get them to correct me —
> correcting is easier than specifying.

**5. "A client is unhappy mid-implementation. What do you do?"**
> Call, don't email. Let them say the whole thing before responding —
> interrupting doubles the length of the conversation. Separate what actually
> went wrong from how it feels. Agree one concrete next step with a date.
> Then over-communicate until trust is back. This is essentially retention
> work, which is what I did professionally.

**6. "How do you keep a project on track?"**
> Named owners, real dates, and a short written status that goes out whether
> or not there's good news. Most slippage is invisible until it's expensive;
> a weekly written status makes it visible while it's still cheap.

**7. "How technical are you?"**
> Comfortable with Sheets, Forms, Apps Script with AI assistance, Make.com,
> and I understand APIs and webhooks well enough to document a workflow
> accurately and ask an engineer a sensible question. I'm not an engineer.
> I'd rather be the person who translates between the customer and the
> engineer than pretend to be both.

**8. "Tell me about a difficult customer conversation."**
> Real retention example. Structure: situation → what they actually wanted
> underneath what they said → what I did → outcome.

**9. "How do you document what you deliver?"**
> A handover packet: what was built, how to use it, what to do when it
> breaks, who to contact. I test my own documentation by following it.
> [Reference the P3 SOP sample.]

**10. "Why this role?"**
> It's the intersection of the two things I'm actually good at — customer
> conversations and process. Most roles ask for one or the other.

# PACK 4 — AI EVALUATOR / QA ANALYST

**1. "Why do you think you'd be good at this?"**
> My job in customer service included quality review — judging interactions
> against defined standards and writing assessments other people acted on.
> That's the same core skill: apply a rubric consistently, catch what's
> subtly wrong rather than obviously wrong, and write a reason someone can
> audit.

**2. "How do you stay consistent across hundreds of items?"**
> Re-read the rubric before starting and again periodically. Work in
>45-minute blocks — fatigue destroys consistency and consistency is the
> thing being measured. If I notice my standard drifting, I go back and
> re-check earlier items rather than pretending it didn't happen.

**3. "What do you do when two responses are close?"**
> Go back to the rubric's priority order rather than my preference. Usually:
> safety, then factual accuracy, then instruction-following, then
> completeness, then clarity and tone. And when it's genuinely close, I say
> so in the critique — a well-argued close call is more useful to a reviewer
> than false confidence.

**4. "How do you detect hallucination?"**
> Confident specificity about things that are hard to know is the main
> signal — exact statistics with no named source, precise citations to
> studies that don't quite exist. I check the checkable claims. False
> precision plus an unverifiable prestigious source is a red flag, not a
> green one.

**5. "Do you have an AI or ML background?"**
> No, and I won't pretend otherwise. This isn't model building — it's quality
> judgment against a rubric, which I've done professionally. I know what I
> don't know, and I flag rather than guess.

**6. "How do you handle an ambiguous rubric?"**
> Follow the rubric's stated intent, note the ambiguity in my justification,
> and ask if there's a channel to ask. What I don't do is quietly substitute
> my own standard — that's how a dataset becomes inconsistent.

**7. "What if you disagree with the rubric?"**
> I apply it anyway and note the disagreement if there's a mechanism for
> that. The rubric exists to make many evaluators consistent. My opinion
> isn't the product.

**8. "How fast do you work?"**
> Carefully rather than quickly. I'd rather do fewer items correctly than
> more items inconsistently, because inconsistent data is worse than less
> data.

**9. "Give an example of catching something subtle."**
> [Use the Q9-style example.] "A response explained VLOOKUP and XLOOKUP
> accurately, then stated XLOOKUP is Excel-only — which is wrong, Google
> Sheets supports it. One wrong clause in four correct sentences. That's the
> kind of thing that gets missed when you skim."

**10. "How do you use AI in your own work?"**
> For drafting and structure, then I verify everything. In evaluation work
> specifically, I don't use it at all to generate my judgments — that would
> defeat the purpose of hiring a human and I understand it's grounds for
> removal.

# PACK 5 — CUSTOMER EXPERIENCE OPERATIONS

**1. "How do you identify why customers churn?"**
> Stated reason and real reason are usually different. Price is what people
> say when the product stopped being worth it. I'd look at contact history
> before the cancellation — most churn has a visible trail of unresolved
> friction weeks earlier.

**2. "Walk me through a retention save."**
> [Real example.] Structure: what they said → what I worked out they actually
> wanted → what I offered → outcome. The point to land: I diagnosed rather
> than discounted.

**3. "How would you reduce support volume?"**
> Count the contacts by theme first — most teams have never actually done
> this. Then rank by volume times handling time, divided by effort to fix.
> The top themes are usually two or three things fixable at the source. I
> built exactly this analysis as a work sample. [Reference P6.]

**4. "What metrics matter in support?"**
> Resolution rate and repeat-contact rate more than handle time. Optimizing
> handle time creates repeat contacts, which costs more overall. And CSAT is
> only useful segmented — an average hides the pattern.

**5. "How do you coach someone whose quality is slipping?"**
> Specific examples, not general feedback. "In this interaction, here's what
> happened and here's what would have worked better." Ask what made it hard —
> often it's a process or tooling gap, not effort. I've been on both sides of
> that conversation.

**6. "What's the biggest mistake support teams make?"**
> Solving the same problem hundreds of times and calling it good service.
> If a question comes in 200 times a month, the answer isn't a better macro,
> it's fixing whatever generates the question.

**7. "How do you handle an angry escalation?"**
> Let them finish. Interrupting doubles the length. Acknowledge the specific
> thing, not the emotion generically — "three days without resolution isn't
> acceptable" beats "I understand your frustration." Then take one concrete
> action with a reference number and a checkable expectation.

**8. "How would you analyze ticket data?"**
> Classify a representative sample by theme, count frequency, estimate
> handling time, look for correlation with churn or repeat contact, then rank
> deflection opportunities by impact over effort. Sample first — you learn
> 80% of it from 100 tickets.

**9. "What tools have you used?"**
> [Real systems from T-Mobile.] Plus Google Workspace and Sheets for
> analysis. I haven't administered Zendesk or Gainsight; I've worked in
> comparable systems and would expect the concepts to transfer.

**10. "Why move from doing support to operating it?"**
> Because I kept seeing the same problems and couldn't fix the cause from
> where I sat. The interesting work is upstream.

## UNIVERSAL RULES

**Always ask at the end — never "no questions":**
1. What does success look like in the first 90 days?
2. What's the biggest challenge facing the team right now?
3. What would make you glad you hired me a year from now?
4. Is there anything about my background that gives you hesitation?

**Question 4 is the highest-value question in this document.** It surfaces
the objection while Steven is still in the room to answer it. Most candidates
never ask it and never learn why they were passed over.

**The three things that must never be said:**
- A skill claimed as unaided when AI did the work
- A job title, client, or certification he doesn't have
- "I'm a perfectionist" as a weakness

**When he doesn't know something:**
> "I haven't done that. Here's the closest thing I have done — [X]. How
> important is it for this role?"

Honest, gives them something real, and turns a dead end into information.
**Bluffing is the only unrecoverable interview error**; not knowing something
is normal and is usually forgiven.
