# Steven's Guide — SOP & Process Document Polish

Fictional demonstration only. This explains what just happened and what it
does and doesn't prove.

## The fictional example, in plain English

Someone wrote down, in a rambling voice-memo style, everything they do to
get an office ready for a new employee's first day: tell IT ahead of time,
get a badge, pack a welcome kit, meet them at the door, watch them log in,
that kind of thing. It repeated itself, jumped around, and left one thing
admittedly unclear (what happens if the new person's first week is remote).

The polished version took all of that and turned it into a real step-by-step
instruction sheet: sections for what it's for, who does it, what you need,
numbered steps grouped by *when* they happen (2 days before, day before,
morning of, during the day, end of day), warnings for the two things most
likely to go wrong (the parking pass takes longer than a day 1 turnaround,
and you have to actually watch the login work, not assume it), a checklist
to tick off, and the one unclear thing was written down as a question for
whoever owns the process — not guessed at.

## 1. What did AI actually do?

Drafted the messy fictional raw notes, restructured them into the SOP
format (headings, numbered steps, warnings, checklist), built the actual
Word and PDF files, and produced a line-by-line reconciliation table
checking every fact against the source.

## 2. What would Steven personally do on a real job?

- Read the customer's raw material fully himself before AI touches it, to
  screen for the refusal categories below.
- Read the finished SOP against the source material himself — not just
  trust AI's own reconciliation table — before sending it to the customer.
- Decide, personally, whether an unclear item gets flagged as a
  clarification question rather than let AI quietly resolve it.
- Communicate directly with the customer about anything flagged, and about
  the one revision included in the offer.

## 3. What information must the customer provide?

All the facts, even if messy — dates, names of tools/sheets/systems
involved, contact points, timing, and any known exceptions or gaps. The
service organizes what's given; it does not fill in what's missing.

## 4. What must Steven verify?

- That every fact in the output actually came from the customer's material
  (no invented steps) — using a reconciliation pass like `04` on every
  real job, not just this demo.
- That anything genuinely unclear in the source is flagged as a question,
  not resolved by guessing.
- That the document opens and edits correctly and the PDF isn't clipped or
  broken — a quick visual check every time, not assumed from one clean test.

## 5. What could go wrong?

- **Incomplete source material.** This fictional demo was built so every
  needed fact was already present — a deliberate design choice for testing
  the "don't invent" rule cleanly. A real customer's notes may have many
  more gaps than the one intentionally left here, which means more
  clarification questions and possibly a source too incomplete to
  responsibly finish for $99.
- **Scope drift.** A customer may expect Steven to *improve* or *fix* the
  process, not just organize what they gave him — that's process
  consulting, outside this offer, and must be declined or clarified upfront.
- **Hidden sensitive content.** A process that looks ordinary at a glance
  (like an office SOP) could turn out to touch safety, medical, financial,
  or compliance decisions once actually read. Steven must screen the
  material himself before starting, not after.
- **Format surprises.** This demo used clean typed notes. A real submission
  could be an audio transcript, a scanned PDF, or a spreadsheet — each
  needs a different intake step AI can help with, but Steven should expect
  the first few real jobs to take longer than this demo suggests.

## 6. What would require asking the customer a question?

Any step where the source material states a rule but leaves a condition
undefined (like this demo's "what if the first week is remote/hybrid"),
any place two parts of the source seem to contradict each other, and any
place the source implies a fact without stating it plainly enough to write
as an instruction.

## 7. What type of SOP jobs should Steven REFUSE?

- Anything touching legal, compliance, or regulatory procedure.
- Anything touching medical or clinical procedure.
- Anything touching financial procedure with real money/compliance stakes.
- Anything safety-critical (equipment operation, hazardous materials,
  anything where a wrong step could hurt someone).
- Any request to invent, design, or improve a business process rather than
  organize an existing one.
- Any request that amounts to ISO consulting, operations strategy, or
  process-engineering advice.
- Any source material so incomplete or contradictory that finishing it
  responsibly would require guessing more than clarifying.

## 8. Could Steven recognize a bad output himself?

**Yes, for the failure modes this demo actually tests:** a missing fact,
an invented fact, an unclear step written as if it were certain, a broken
document, or a clipped PDF are all things Steven can check by reading the
source and the output side by side — the reconciliation-table method in
`04-FACT-RECONCILIATION.md` is simple enough for him to redo by hand on a
real job, not something only AI can verify.

**Not yet proven:** whether Steven can reliably *screen intake* for hidden
scope problems (a safety-adjacent process disguised as routine admin, a
customer's implicit expectation of process improvement rather than
organizing) before starting work. That's a judgment call under real
customer pressure, not something this fictional, pre-screened example
tested.

---

## CAPABILITY TEST: **PASS**

The core mechanic — organize without inventing, flag instead of guess,
produce a clean editable/exportable document — worked correctly on this
example and was independently checked, not just asserted.

## READY FOR LIVE BUYER TEST: **YES, conditional**

Not an unconditional green light. Before any real customer submission,
Steven needs a written personal-intake checklist he actually reads before
accepting a job: confirm it's an ordinary administrative/procedural
process (not legal/medical/financial/safety-critical), confirm the ask is
"organize what I give you" not "fix/design my process," and set an
expectation with the customer that unclear items get flagged as questions,
not resolved by guessing. With that checklist in place, this narrow
service — a plain administrative SOP, source material provided, output
organized and flagged rather than invented — is safe for Steven to deliver
with AI assistance.
