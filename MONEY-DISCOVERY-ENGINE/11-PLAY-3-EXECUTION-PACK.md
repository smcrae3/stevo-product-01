# 11 — PLAY 3 EXECUTION PACK: CONTRACT OPERATIONS / SOP WRITER

## POSITIONING
Someone who can sit with the person who does the work, understand it, and
write it down so the next person can do it correctly. Not a technical
writer with a CS degree — an operations person who communicates unusually
well.

## ONE-SENTENCE OFFER
> I turn undocumented processes that live in one person's head into SOPs a
> new hire can follow on day one.

## BUYER PROFILE
SMBs, startups, agencies, healthcare/logistics/field-service ops teams
hiring **part-time, contract, or fractional** remote staff. Titles to
target: SOP Writer, Process Documentation Specialist, Operations Assistant,
Operations Coordinator, Implementation Specialist, Onboarding Coordinator,
Business Systems Assistant, RevOps Assistant.

## QUALIFICATION RULES — apply if
- Remote, part-time or contract acceptable.
- Rate ≥ $18/hr (ops) or ≥ $28/hr (SOP/documentation).
- Requirements are **process/communication-weighted**, not
  engineering-weighted.
- No hard requirement for a degree or certification Steven lacks.

**Skip:** roles demanding 5+ years in a named enterprise system; anything
requiring a clearance or license; commission-only; "unpaid trial period";
anything asking Steven to pay for training or equipment.

**Apply anyway if it's 70% match.** Job descriptions are wish lists.
Under-applying is Steven's actual failure mode here, not over-applying.

## PRICING (confirmed market data, E4)
| Role | Market rate |
|---|---|
| Remote SOP / process technical writer | **$38.94/hr avg; $28.85-$47.12 band** |
| Operations assistant, remote | **$19.82/hr avg; $15.38-$22.36 band** |
| Implementation / onboarding support | ~$20-30/hr |

**Ask band:** $30-38/hr for documentation roles, $20-24/hr for ops roles.
Do not anchor low — the published data supports these numbers, and quoting
$15/hr for SOP work actively signals inexperience.

## DELIVERY SCOPE
Process interviews, SOP writing, work-instruction docs, training guides,
knowledge-base articles, process maps, operational admin, reporting support.

## OUT OF SCOPE
Engineering, systems administration, regulated/compliance documentation
requiring certification, anything where a documentation error creates legal
or safety exposure Steven can't assess.

## DELIVERY SOP (once hired — this is what makes him keepable)
1. **Watch the process before writing about it.** Screen-share, recorded.
2. **Interview the doer, not the manager.** The manager describes the
   process as designed; the doer knows the exceptions. The exceptions are
   what the SOP is actually for.
3. Ask: "What goes wrong most often?" and "What would you tell a new person
   on day one that isn't written anywhere?" These two questions produce the
   most valuable content in any SOP.
4. **AI first draft** from the transcript.
5. **Verification pass** — walk the steps yourself. Every one.
6. Structure: Purpose → When to use → Prerequisites → Numbered steps with
   screenshots → Exceptions & edge cases → Who to escalate to → Last updated.
7. **Test it on someone who doesn't know the process.** If they get stuck,
   the SOP is wrong — not the reader.
8. Ship with a change log.

## QUALITY CHECKLIST
- [ ] Could a competent new hire follow this with zero verbal help?
- [ ] Are the exceptions documented, not just the happy path?
- [ ] Is every screenshot current and correctly annotated?
- [ ] Named owner and escalation path for every step?
- [ ] Is any step actually three steps in disguise?
- [ ] Did I walk it myself, or just transcribe what I was told?
- [ ] Date and version present?

## SAMPLE / PROOF PLAN
**The same 2 samples as Play 2.** One SOP, one QA/bug sheet. Host them as
PDFs in a shareable Drive folder and link the folder in every application.

Most applicants for these roles submit a résumé and nothing else. A link to
two real, well-made artifacts is a disproportionate advantage — it converts
"claims he can write SOPs" into "here is an SOP he wrote."

## APPLICATION APPROACH
**5 applications per weekday.** Non-negotiable, timeboxed to 45 minutes.
AI tailors each one in ~5 minutes; Steven reads before sending.

**Cover-letter opening that works** (specific, not generic):
> Most process documentation fails because it's written by someone who
> never did the work. I spent years in customer service and retention —
> which is where you find out fast what the written process leaves out. I
> write SOPs by watching the person who actually does the job, then testing
> the document on someone who doesn't. Two samples: [link].

## INTERVIEW PREP — the three questions that will decide it
**"Walk me through how you'd document a process you know nothing about."**
→ Watch it done. Interview the doer, not the manager. Draft. Walk the steps
myself. Test it on a naive reader. Ship with a change log.

**"Tell me about a time you improved a process."** → Use a real CSR example.
Structure: what was broken → what I noticed → what I changed → what it
saved. Have two of these written out before the first interview.

**"How technical are you?"** → *Answer honestly; it tests calibration, not
skill.* "Comfortable in Google Sheets, Forms, and Apps Script with AI
assistance, and I understand APIs and webhooks well enough to document a
workflow accurately. I'm not an engineer, and I'd rather tell you that now
than discover it in week three."

## OBJECTION RESPONSES
**"You don't have technical writing experience."** → "Not by that title. I
have years of explaining complicated things to people who were already
frustrated, which is the harder version of the same skill. The samples show
the output — I'd rather be judged on those than on a job title."

**"This is a full-time role."** → "Understood. If a contract or part-time
version ever opens, I'd like to be considered. Would it help if I sent a
sample now so I'm on file?" *(Costs nothing, occasionally works.)*

**"Why are you leaving customer service?"** → "I'm not leaving what I'm good
at — I'm applying it upstream. Fixing the same problem 200 times teaches you
where the process is broken. I'd rather document and fix it than keep
apologizing for it."

## SUCCESS / FAILURE CRITERIA
- **Week 1:** samples built; 15+ applications submitted.
- **Week 2:** 25 total applications; 1+ response.
- **Week 4:** 50 total; 1+ interview.
- **Week 8:** offer or a clear read on why not.
- **Calibration:** a 2-5% application-to-interview rate is *normal*. Twenty
  silent applications is not evidence of failure. **Two hundred** silent
  applications means the résumé or the targeting is wrong — fix the input,
  don't increase the volume.
