## Turning an Informal Customer-Inquiry Process Into a Repeatable SOP

### THE PROBLEM

A small service business ("Harbor Line Services," fictional) takes customer
inquiries through a web form, a shared inbox, and phone messages. No single
person owns the process. Whoever notices an email first handles it, or
doesn't. There's no shared definition of "urgent," no record of what's
outstanding, and no way for a new hire to learn the process except by
watching someone else do it inconsistently for a few weeks.

This is not a hypothetical failure mode — it's the default state of almost
every small business that grew past "the owner personally reads every
email." The cost isn't visible in any single dropped inquiry; it's visible
in the pattern of similar failures no one connects, because nothing is
written down to connect them.

### THE APPROACH

1. Start from a recorded walkthrough (in a real engagement) or a structured
   interview of whoever currently does the work — not a guess at how the
   process "should" work.
2. Ask specifically what goes wrong most often and what a new person would
   need to know that isn't written anywhere. These two questions produce the
   exceptions section, which is where most of a good SOP's value lives.
3. Draft with AI assistance for structure and first-pass language.
4. **Walk every step personally** against the actual tools involved (in this
   demonstration, a Google Sheet and Gmail) before treating any step as
   verified.
5. Test the draft on a reader unfamiliar with the process. Wherever they get
   stuck, the document is wrong — not the reader.

### THE DELIVERABLE

A single SOP document (`01-SOP-DEMONSTRATION.md`) covering intake through
closure, plus a companion process map (`02-SOP-PROCESS-MAP.md`) showing the
happy path, the exception path, and the escalation path as one connected
flow.

### WHAT WAS STANDARDIZED

- A single, unambiguous urgency classification with a stated default
  direction for ambiguous cases (round up, not down)
- A fixed set of status values, replacing free-text notes that couldn't be
  reported on
- Ownership assignment logic based on subject area, not "whoever's available"
- A concrete auto-close rule (3 business days of silence after resolution)
  so records don't accumulate indefinitely in limbo

### WHAT RISKS WERE REDUCED

- **The unassigned-record risk** — the single most common failure mode in
  informal intake processes, where an inquiry is technically "in the
  system" but nobody is accountable for it
- **The inconsistent-urgency risk** — different staff classifying the same
  situation differently depending on mood, workload, or how the customer's
  message happened to read
- **The knowledge-loss risk** — if the one person who "just knows how it
  works" leaves, the process leaves with them

### WHAT STEVEN'S ROLE WOULD BE

In a real engagement: conducting the interview (or reviewing a recorded
walkthrough), asking the two questions that surface exceptions, personally
verifying every step against the live system, testing the document on a
naive reader, and formatting the final SOP to the client's own template and
brand.

### WHAT AI ASSISTED WITH

Initial structural drafting from interview notes or a transcript, and
tightening prose for clarity. AI did not generate the exceptions, the
escalation logic, or the specific field/status naming — those require
understanding the actual process, which is exactly the part a transcript
alone doesn't supply.

### WHAT HUMAN QA WAS REQUIRED

Every step in the procedure was checked for internal consistency (does each
decision point have exactly one path forward for every possible answer?),
and the terminology was checked for consistency throughout (the same status
value, the same field names, used identically everywhere they appear — a
common failure in AI-assisted drafts is quietly renaming the same concept
partway through).

### WHAT THIS DEMONSTRATION PROVES

That the output can hold together as an actual usable procedure: every
decision point resolves, every exception path rejoins the main flow, the
escalation logic is distinct from and faster than normal routing, and the
document reads like something a real business could adopt with light
editing for its own specifics.

### WHAT IT DOES NOT PROVE

That Steven has documented a process for a paying client, that any real
business has adopted this specific SOP, or that this exact structure fits
every process — a genuinely more complex or more regulated process would
need a different shape. It also does not demonstrate speed under real
client deadline pressure, since no deadline pressure existed here.
