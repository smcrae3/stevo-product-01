# 01 — Final Script

Target length: 4–7 minutes. Not a sales-results video — Steven has not
made money from this service yet, and the script never implies otherwise.
No client identity, no proprietary list, nothing implying this was paid
work — every example below is Steven's own public-data practice,
independently confirmed by real searches this session (sources listed at
the end of this file).

---

## HOOK (0:00–0:30)

**SAY:**
"AI can find information incredibly fast. It can also hand you something
that's completely wrong — and say it with total confidence. So here's the
rule I use every time I do research with AI: AI discovers, the source
proves it, and I'm the one who approves it. Let me show you what that
actually looks like."

**SCREEN:** Title card or simple text on screen: "AI DISCOVERS. SOURCE
PROVES. YOU APPROVE."

---

## BODY

### 1. The research task (0:30–1:00)

**SAY:**
"Here's a simple version of a real research task: for a list of small
public water utilities, find who's actually in charge of running the
plant, their published contact info, and a phone number — with a source
link proving every fact. Nothing invented, nothing guessed."

**SCREEN:** A simple spreadsheet header row: utility name, city, state,
contact_name, contact_title, email, phone, source_url, notes.

### 2. What AI does (1:00–1:45)

**SAY:**
"AI is genuinely useful for the first part — it can search fast, and
point me toward the city's water department page instead of me digging
through ten menus myself."

**SCREEN:** Show a real search query and a real result — e.g., searching
for a city's water department page and AI surfacing the right link.

### 3. What the human checks (1:45–2:30)

**SAY:**
"But here's the part that matters: I don't take AI's word for what's on
that page. I open it myself."

**SCREEN:** Show clicking through to the actual source page —
`watertown-ny.gov/departments/WaterDepartment` — and visibly reading the
name and email printed on the page.

**SAY (over the page):**
"This one's easy — Watertown, New York. Water Superintendent, name and
email, right there on the official page. That's a clean verified record."

### 4. Why guessing email addresses is dangerous (2:30–3:15)

**SAY:**
"Here's where people get into trouble. If I don't find a published
email, it's really tempting to just guess — firstname.lastname at the
city's domain, something that looks plausible. I don't do that. A wrong
guess can bounce, or worse, go to the wrong person entirely. If it's not
actually published, it doesn't go in the spreadsheet as fact."

**SCREEN:** Simple on-screen text: "NEVER GUESS AN EMAIL."

### 5. Example where a direct email is unavailable (3:15–4:00)

**SAY:**
"Case in point — Edgartown, Massachusetts. Their water department page
names the superintendent and gives a phone number. No email listed
anywhere on that page. So the honest answer isn't to invent one — it's to
leave that field blank, use the phone number I actually found, and note
that no email was published."

**SCREEN:** Show the real Edgartown water department page, with the
phone number visible and no email present — and the spreadsheet row
showing the email field intentionally blank, with a note explaining why.

### 6. Source URL verification (4:00–4:30)

**SAY:**
"Every single row gets the exact page it came from. Not 'I found this on
their website' — the actual link. That way anyone can check my work in
ten seconds, including me, a week from now."

**SCREEN:** The source_url column, populated for both example rows.

### 7. Final spreadsheet (4:30–4:50)

**SAY:**
"Here's what the finished, small example looks like — a couple of
complete records, and one where I was honest that the information just
isn't public."

**SCREEN:** Wide shot of the small demonstration spreadsheet, both rows
visible side by side.

### 8. Three-row QA result (4:50–5:15)

**SAY:**
"Before I'd call any of this done, I go back and check: does the source
still load? Does the name actually appear on the page? Is the email
copy-pasted, not guessed? That's the actual QA step — not optional,
every time."

**SCREEN:** A short checklist on screen: Source opens? / Name on page? /
Email published, not guessed? / Source linked?

---

## CONCLUSION (5:15–5:50)

**SAY:**
"The takeaway isn't 'AI does the research for you.' It doesn't. The
takeaway is: AI makes the *discovery* part faster. The *verification*
part is still entirely on you. That one habit — never trusting a fact
until you've opened the source yourself — is the difference between
research that's actually useful and research that just sounds
confident."

**SCREEN:** Text on screen: "AI DISCOVERS. SOURCE PROVES. YOU APPROVE."

"If you want more practical, no-hype AI workflows like this, subscribe —
that's what this channel's about."
